How to organise an architecture design doc

You have the design in your head. This page helps you decide what goes on page one, which diagram comes next, and what can wait. It includes eight ready-made outlines, one for each common kind of design doc. Diagram names are the standard ones (“sequence”, “container”, “DFD”). The diagram catalogue explains each one.

Hover over a diagram badge, or tap it, to see that diagram type.

A design doc is an argument, not an inventory.

You are not listing everything you built. You are walking a reader through a case. Put each section where the reader’s next question would come up. Before you place a diagram, ask: “Has the reader asked the question this answers yet?” If not, move it later.

The usual order

Most design docs move through six phases, from the big picture down to the details. This is common practice, not a formal standard; the closest published template is arc42. The reason is simple: a reader needs to see a box before they can follow an arrow into it. Three of the outlines below keep this order. The other five change it on purpose, and each one says why.

  1. WHYthe problem, the choice, and what we rejectedprose only
  2. WHOwho uses it, and what it talks to
  3. WHATthe parts, and what is inside one
  4. HOWthe flow, the logic, the data
  5. WHEREinfrastructure and environments
  6. WHAT IFfailure, rollout, open questionsprose

Widest first. Don’t open on the deployment diagram (a runbook is the exception, see outline 8). Nobody has asked “where” yet.

Five ordering rules why that order

RuleWhy
1Big picture first. Show the whole system, then its parts, then the inside of one part. Never the reverse. Say your second section mentions orders.pricing (the pricing code inside the Orders service). The reader doesn’t know there is an Orders service yet. Now they have to remember a name nobody explained.
2Parts before steps. Show what the parts are before you show what they do. Every box in a sequence diagram should be something the reader has already seen. If not, each one is a new name to learn in the middle of a flow.
3The decision before the design. Say what you chose, and what you rejected, before you show how it works. Reviewers push back on the decision, not the diagrams. If the decision is on page 7, they read six pages before they reach the part they care about.
4Infrastructure late. The deployment diagram comes after you explain how the system behaves (a runbook is the exception, see outline 8). “Where does it run?” only matters once the reader knows what “it” is. A cloud diagram on page one answers a question nobody has asked.
5At most one diagram per section. If a section needs two diagrams, it is covering two topics. Split it into two sections.

Eight outlines, by use case pick the nearest, then cut

Pick the outline closest to your doc, then delete what you don’t need. Each row is a section. The tag on the right names the diagram for that section; — means prose or a table. The shaded row is the most important section. If someone reads only one section, it should be that one.

1 · New system design

greenfield · 6–12 pages · eng + reviewers

  1. The design in one pagethe whole argument, standalone—
  2. Problem and why nowwhat breaks if we do nothing—
  3. Who is involvedusers and systems around it
  4. Constraintsbudget, latency, compliance, team—
  5. The decisionwhat we will build, in 3 sentences—
  6. Alternatives rejectedand the one fact that killed each—
  7. The partsdeployables and stores
  8. Inside the main partmodules and their calls
  9. The main flowwhat happens on a request
  10. Data modelwhat we store and how it relates
  11. Where it runsservices, boundaries, cost
  12. When it breaksfailure modes and who gets paged
  13. Rolloutphases, flags, the point of no return—
  14. Open questionsnamed owner, named date—

Works because the reviewer can stop after section 6 and still have reviewed the real decision. Section 3 comes before the decision, because the constraints and the decision name those users and systems.

Fails when sections 7–11 come before 5. The design then looks already decided, and reviewers feel they can no longer change it.

2 · Feature / change design

brownfield · 2–4 pages · the team

  1. What changesone paragraph, no preamble—
  2. Todaythe part you are about to touch
  3. Afterthe same diagram, changes marked
  4. The new flowonly the path that differs
  5. Data changesnew fields, backfill, old readers
  6. Risk and rollbackhow we undo it, and how fast—
  7. Testswhat proves it works—

The trick: sections 2 and 3 are the same diagram twice, with the changes highlighted in the second. The reader sees the change at a glance.

Fails when “today” is redrawn from scratch. Then the reader compares two drawings instead of reading one change.

3 · Security / access control

10–20 pages · security + business owner

  1. The design in one pagefor the owner who reads nothing else—
  2. The decisionwhere enforcement lives, and why there—
  3. Who reads whatroles against fields
  4. Which records they reachscope, separate from fields
  5. Decisions the business ownsthe questions you cannot answer—
  6. Alternatives rejectedand why each one leaks—
  7. What is exposed todayevery field, every surface
  8. Enforcement in the codethe one place that decides
  9. Trust boundarieswhat crosses, in which direction
  10. Tests that prove ita leak test, not a unit test—
  11. The request flowlogin, permission check, response
  12. Where it runsgateway, compute, stores
  13. Audit and operationsone line per request, metadata only—
  14. Rolloutwho gets it first, and how you revoke—

Works because it splits into decide (1–6), build (7–10) and ship (11–14). Business owners can stop after 6. Engineers build from 7–10. Whoever ships it reads 11–14.

Fails when the roles-and-fields matrix is written as prose. A reviewer needs to check one field against one role, row by row.

4 · Migration / replatform

5–10 pages · eng + whoever owns the risk

  1. Why movethe cost of staying, in numbers—
  2. Todaythe system as it is now
  3. Targetsame detail, same layout, new parts
  4. Gaprow per capability: keep, port, drop—
  5. Cutoverthe exact order of operations
  6. Data migrationbackfill, dual-write, reconciliation
  7. Rollbackat each step, and the last reversible one
  8. Environmentswhere you rehearse it
  9. Phases and dateswho does what, when—

The trick: draw “today” and “target” at the same level of detail with the same layout. Then the differences stand out without any searching. Rollback (7) comes right after cutover and data migration, before environments, so each step sits next to its undo.

Fails when rollback is one sentence. Name the last step you can still undo. Otherwise nobody knows when going back stops being an option.

5 · Third-party integration

3–6 pages · eng + the vendor’s engineer

  1. What we need from themfirst, because it has the longest lead time—
  2. Contextthem as one external box
  3. The contractendpoints, payloads, limits, errors—
  4. The handshakeauth and the first real call
  5. What crosses the boundarywhich fields leave, and to whom
  6. Failure modestheir 500, their timeout, their rate limit
  7. Credentialswhere stored, who rotates, how often—
  8. Cost per calland the ceiling that stops a runaway—

Works because section 1 is a request, not a summary. Getting access from a vendor can take weeks, so ask on day one. This is why the outline starts here, not with the problem.

Fails when section 5 is missing. Then nobody tracks which customer data goes to the vendor, until an auditor asks.

6 · Data platform / pipeline

4–8 pages · data + the consumers

  1. Who consumes this, and for whatstart at the output, not the source—
  2. Sourcesowner, freshness, volume, trust—
  3. Where each field comes fromsource to output, field level
  4. The modelwhat one row means, measures, lookups
  5. The jobswhich job runs after which, and when
  6. Quality gateswhat fails the run, what only warns
  7. Where it runscompute, storage, cost per run
  8. Backfill and reprocessinghow you fix a bad day—

Works because it starts at the output and works back to the sources. That is the order the people who use the data care about.

Fails when it opens on the job graph (DAG). The jobs mean nothing until the reader knows what the data is for.

7 · ADR — one decision

1 page · future you · never longer

  1. Title, status, dateproposed · accepted · superseded by N—
  2. Contextwhat was true at the time—
  3. Decision“We will …” — one sentence, active voice—
  4. Consequencesgood and bad, both stated—
  5. Alternativesone line each, with the disqualifying fact—

Works because you never edit an old ADR (architecture decision record). You write a new one that replaces it. In two years, the trail of decisions is still readable. The first four sections follow Michael Nygard’s ADR format. Alternatives are added at the end, so the format stays intact.

Fails when it grows diagrams. One at most, usually none. If you need more, write outline 1 instead.

8 · Handover / runbook

3–5 pages · whoever is on call at 3 a.m.

  1. What this system doestwo sentences, no jargon—
  2. What it looks likethe one picture to orient on
  3. Normalthe numbers that mean “fine”—
  4. The alertsone block each: cause, check, fix
  5. How to deploy and roll backexact commands, copy-pasteable
  6. Accesswhat you need, and who grants it—
  7. Who to escalate toby role, with a fallback—

Works because someone who didn’t build the system can act on every section at 3 a.m. That is also why the deployment diagram comes second: on call, “where does it run?” is the first question.

Fails when it explains the design. Nobody debugs from a component diagram at 3 a.m. Put that in outline 1 and link to it.

What a reviewer actually reads

Most reviewers skim. They read these parts, in this order, and many stop early. Arrange the doc for them, not in the order you worked things out.

OrderWhat they readWhat it means for you
1The titleName the topic plainly, such as “Checkout pricing”. Put the claim in the summary’s first sentence: “We will calculate prices on the server, not in the checkout page.”
2The one-page summaryWrite it last, and make it survive alone. Many readers read only this.
3The first diagramMake it the big-picture one. If the first diagram shows code modules, the reviewer has nothing to hang them on.
4The decisions and alternativesThis is where they push back. Put it before the design, so they don’t read 10 pages first.
5The risks and open questionsThey look for what you are worried about. If nothing is listed, they assume you have not looked.
6Everything else, if at allSections 7 onward exist for the person who implements it, not the person who approves it.

Figure, table or prose?

UseWhenBecause
A figureThe content is a shape: what contains what, what calls what, what happens in what order.A reader takes in a shape in one glance and a paragraph in thirty seconds.
A tableThe content is a set of rows a reviewer must check one at a time — roles, fields, endpoints, costs.A reviewer can check a table row by row. Nobody can check a list of fields drawn as boxes.
ProseThe content is a judgement: why you chose this, what you rejected, what worries you.Reasoning has no shape. Turning an argument into a diagram makes it harder to follow, not easier.
NothingIt is true of every system, or a tool can generate it.“We will write tests” tells the reader nothing new. A hand-drawn dependency graph goes out of date. Both waste the reader’s time.

Five ways the order goes wrong

  1. The doc opens on the deployment diagram. Usually because it was the first thing the author drew. It answers “where”, and the reader has not yet asked. Move it to the WHERE phase and open on the system context diagram (a runbook is the exception, see outline 8).
  2. The decision is on page 7. The pages before it read as if everything is already settled. Reviewers who disagree have to argue against six pages of design.
  3. Alternatives are listed without the disqualifying fact. “We considered Kafka” is not a rejection. “Kafka needs a team to run it, and we have two engineers” is.
  4. Steps before parts. A sequence diagram on page 2 shows boxes that only get explained on page 5. The reader meets each one as an unknown name.
  5. The summary was written first. It then describes the doc you intended, not the one you wrote. Write it last, every time.

What is standard here, and what is not. The diagram names are standard: they come from UML 2.5, C4, ArchiMate and the data flow diagram (DFD) convention used in STRIDE threat modelling, and the diagram catalogue has a card for each. The section orders are not a named standard — they follow common practice. Two exceptions: outline 7 builds on Michael Nygard’s ADR format, and the closest published template to outline 1 is arc42. Treat the rest as a starting shape to cut down, not a specification to follow.
