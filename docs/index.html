How to organise an architecture design doc

Which diagram comes first, which comes last, and why. Then eight skeletons — one per kind of document — showing the section order and what diagram belongs in each. Every diagram is named the way other people name it — “sequence”, “container”, “DFD” — and the diagram catalogue has a card for each one.

Click a diagram badge to preview that diagram type.

A design doc is an argument, not an inventory.

Sections go in the order the reader’s questions arise, and each diagram answers a question the previous section raised. If a diagram answers nothing anyone asked yet, it is too early. That one test settles almost every ordering argument.

The usual order

Six phases. This ordering is common practice, not a named standard — the closest published template is arc42. Every doc below is a subset of this order, never a reshuffle of it. The movement is wide to narrow: a reader must meet a box before they can follow an arrow into it.

Five ordering rules why that order

RuleWhy
1Widest altitude first. Context → container → component, never the reverse. A reader who meets orders.pricing before they know the system exists has to hold an unexplained name in working memory. Zooming in is free; zooming out mid-document is not.
2Structure before behaviour. Every structure diagram before any behaviour diagram. A sequence diagram’s participants have to be things the reader already met. Otherwise each lifeline is a new noun arriving at the worst moment.
3The decision before the design. State what you chose, and what you rejected, before you draw how it works. Reviewers argue with the decision, not the drawing. Burying it on page 7 wastes the first six pages of their attention.
4Deployment late. The deployment diagram belongs after the behaviour, not before it. “Where does it run” only matters once “what is it” is settled. A doc that opens on a cloud diagram has answered a question nobody asked.
5One diagram per section, at most. Two diagrams in one section means the section has two topics. Split the section, not the figure.

All eight at once 23 sections · 8 documents

Every section any of the eight documents uses, down the side. Every document, across the top. A solid block means that document has that section; a dark block marks the one section the document exists to deliver; a faint dotted line means it is absent. The block’s shape says what fills it — two bars for prose, a grid for a table or matrix, and for a figure, a small silhouette of that diagram type, named in the key under the matrix. Read a column to see the shape of one document; read a row to see which documents need that section at all.

Section names are canonical, not verbatim: a few are merged onto one row where two documents call the same thing by different names. The highlighted column, security / access control, shows the decide-build-ship order most clearly. The decision and its table come before any diagram, the diagrams follow, and rollout comes last: decide, then build, then ship.

Eight skeletons, by use case pick the nearest, then cut

Each row is a section. The chip on the right names the diagram that belongs there, or — for prose and tables only. The shaded row is the section the whole document exists to deliver. If a reader reads one section, make it that one.

1 · New system design

greenfield · 6–12 pages · eng + reviewers

  1. The design in one pagethe whole argument, standalone—
  2. Problem and why nowwhat breaks if we do nothing—
  3. Who is involvedusers and systems around itcontext
  4. Constraintsbudget, latency, compliance, team—
  5. The decisionwhat we will build, in 3 sentences—
  6. Alternatives rejectedand the one fact that killed each—
  7. The partsdeployables and storescontainer
  8. Inside the main partmodules and their callscomponent
  9. The main flowwhat happens on a requestsequence
  10. Data modelwhat we store and how it relatesERD
  11. Where it runsservices, boundaries, costdeployment
  12. When it breaksfailure modes and who gets pagedrunbook
  13. Rolloutphases, flags, the point of no return—
  14. Open questionsnamed owner, named date—

Works because the reviewer can stop after section 6 and still have reviewed the real decision.

Fails when sections 7–11 arrive before 5. Then every page of design is read as a fait accompli.

2 · Feature / change design

brownfield · 2–4 pages · the team

  1. What changesone paragraph, no preamble—
  2. Todaythe part you are about to touchcomponent
  3. Afterthe same diagram, changes markedcomponent
  4. The new flowonly the path that differsactivity
  5. Data changesnew fields, backfill, old readerslineage
  6. Risk and rollbackhow we undo it, and how fast—
  7. Testswhat proves it works—

The trick: sections 2 and 3 are the same diagram twice, at the same altitude, with the delta highlighted. Nothing else communicates a change so fast.

Fails when “today” is redrawn from scratch. Then the reader compares two drawings instead of reading one change.

3 · Security / access control

10–20 pages · security + business owner

  1. The design in one pagefor the owner who reads nothing else—
  2. The decisionwhere enforcement lives, and why there—
  3. Who reads whatroles against fieldsmatrix
  4. Which records they reachscope, separate from fieldsmatrix
  5. Decisions the business ownsthe questions you cannot answer—
  6. Alternatives rejectedand why each one leaks—
  7. What is exposed todayevery field, every surfacelineage
  8. Enforcement in the codethe one place that decidesactivity
  9. Trust boundarieswhat crosses, in which directionDFD
  10. Tests that prove ita leak test, not a unit test—
  11. The request flowlogin, permission check, responsesequence
  12. Where it runsgateway, compute, storesdeployment
  13. Audit and operationsone line per request, metadata only—
  14. Rolloutwho gets it first, and how you revoke—

Works because it splits decide (1–6), build (7–10) and ship (11–14). Three audiences, three stopping points.

Fails when the field matrix is prose. A reviewer must be able to check one row against one role.

4 · Migration / replatform

5–10 pages · eng + whoever owns the risk

  1. Why movethe cost of staying, in numbers—
  2. Todaythe system as it is nowcontainer
  3. Targetsame altitude, same layout, new partscontainer
  4. Gaprow per capability: keep, port, drop—
  5. Cutoverthe exact order of operationssequence
  6. Data migrationbackfill, dual-write, reconciliationDAG
  7. Rollbackat each step, and the last reversible oneactivity
  8. Environmentswhere you rehearse itenvironments
  9. Phases and dateswho does what, when—

The trick: draw “today” and “target” at the same altitude with the same layout, so the eye reads the difference instead of hunting for it.

Fails when rollback is one sentence. Name the last reversible step, or the plan is a one-way door nobody noticed.

5 · Third-party integration

3–6 pages · eng + the vendor’s engineer

  1. What we need from themfirst, because it has the longest lead time—
  2. Contextthem as one external boxcontext
  3. The contractendpoints, payloads, limits, errors—
  4. The handshakeauth and the first real callsequence
  5. Failure modestheir 500, their timeout, their rate limitactivity
  6. What crosses the boundarywhich fields leave, and to whomDFD
  7. Credentialswhere stored, who rotates, how often—
  8. Cost per calland the ceiling that stops a runaway—

Works because section 1 is an ask, not a summary. Vendor access takes weeks; start it on day one.

Fails when section 6 is missing. Nobody notices which customer fields leave the building until an auditor asks.

6 · Data platform / pipeline

4–8 pages · data + the consumers

  1. Who consumes this, and for whatstart at the output, not the source—
  2. Sourcesowner, freshness, volume, trust—
  3. Where each field comes fromsource to output, field levellineage
  4. The modelgrain, facts, dimensionsstar schema
  5. The jobsorder, parallelism, scheduleDAG
  6. Quality gateswhat fails the run, what only warnsactivity
  7. Where it runscompute, storage, cost per rundeployment
  8. Backfill and reprocessinghow you fix a bad day—

Works because it reads backwards: output first, then source. That is the order the consumer cares about.

Fails when it opens on the DAG. A task graph means nothing until the reader knows what the data is for.

7 · ADR — one decision

1 page · future you · never longer

  1. Title, status, dateproposed · accepted · superseded by N—
  2. Contextthe forces that applied at the time—
  3. Decision“We will …” — one sentence, active voice—
  4. Consequencesgood and bad, both stated—
  5. Alternativesone line each, with the disqualifying fact—

Works because it is immutable. You never edit an ADR; you supersede it. That is what makes the history readable in two years.

Fails when it grows a diagram. At most one, and usually none — if you need a figure, you are writing doc type 1.

8 · Handover / runbook

3–5 pages · whoever is on call at 3 a.m.

  1. What this system doestwo sentences, no jargon—
  2. What it looks likethe one picture to orient ondeployment
  3. Normalthe numbers that mean “fine”—
  4. The alertsone block each: cause, check, fixrunbook
  5. How to deploy and roll backexact commands, copy-pasteablepipeline
  6. Accesswhat you need, and who grants it—
  7. Who to escalate toby role, with a fallback—

Works because every section is actionable at 3 a.m. by someone who did not build it.

Fails when it explains architecture. Nobody debugs from a component diagram at 3 a.m. — put that in doc type 1 and link it.

What a reviewer actually reads

In order, and usually in under five minutes. Design the document around this, not around the order you figured things out in.

OrderWhat they readWhat it means for you
1The titleMake it a claim, not a topic. “Calculate prices on the server, not in the checkout page” beats “Pricing design”.
2The one-page summaryWrite it last, and make it survive alone. Many readers read only this.
3The first diagramMake it the widest one. A reviewer who meets a module name here is already lost.
4The decisions and alternativesThis is where they push back. Put it before the design, or they argue after reading 10 pages of it.
5The risks and open questionsThey look for what you are worried about. If nothing is listed, they assume you have not looked.
6Everything else, if at allSections 7 onward exist for the person who implements it, not the person who approves it.

Figure, table or prose?

UseWhenBecause
A figureThe content is a shape: what contains what, what calls what, what happens in what order.A reader takes in a shape in one glance and a paragraph in thirty seconds.
A tableThe content is a set of rows a reviewer must check one at a time — roles, fields, endpoints, costs.Precision. Nobody can audit a field list drawn as boxes.
ProseThe content is a judgement: why you chose this, what you rejected, what worries you.Reasoning has no shape. A diagram of an argument is a flowchart nobody asked for.
NothingIt is true of every system, or a tool can generate it.“We will write tests” and a hand-drawn dependency graph both cost attention and return none.

Five ways the order goes wrong

  1. The doc opens on the deployment diagram. Usually because it was the first thing the author drew. It answers “where”, and the reader has not yet asked. Move it to the WHERE phase and open on the system context diagram.
  2. The decision is on page 7. Every page before it reads as a presentation of something already settled. Reviewers who disagree have to argue backwards.
  3. Alternatives are listed without the disqualifying fact. “We considered Kafka” is not a rejection. “Kafka needs a team to run it, and we have two engineers” is.
  4. Behaviour before structure. A sequence diagram on page 2 whose participants appear on page 5. Every lifeline is an unexplained noun.
  5. The summary was written first. It then describes the doc you intended, not the one you wrote. Write it last, every time.

What is standard here, and what is not. The diagram names are standard: they come from UML 2.5, C4, ArchiMate and the DFD/STRIDE convention, and the diagram catalogue has a card for each. The section orders are not a named standard — they follow common practice. Two exceptions: skeleton 7 is Michael Nygard’s ADR format, and the closest published template to skeleton 1 is arc42. Treat the rest as a starting shape to cut down, not a specification to follow.
